How the model works

The method, the arithmetic, and what it can't do

The main question I wanted to answer was what a player is worth in dollars, to determine what a fair contract value should be. That splits into three parts: impact -> wins -> dollar value. There is some prior work on this for the NBA, particularly the Wins and Dollar Value. The NBA has more advanced tracking data available, leading to things like the DARKO model to measure player impact. The WNBA does not have this data available, so I had to build a model that could measure player impact using only the data that is available.

Plus-minus doesn't work

The simplest measure of impact is plus-minus, the score differential while she's on the floor. If her team outscores opponents by 8 in her 30 minutes, she's +8.

The problem is that a bench player who only ever plays next to three All-Stars will look excellent. A good player on a bad team will look awful. Plus-minus measures the lineup, not the player. What I actually want is her effect holding teammates and opponents constant, and holding things constant is what regression is for.

One coefficient per player

Each row of the data is a single possession: a team gets the ball, something happens, the possession ends. There are about 143,000 of them in the current fit.

The response is points scored on that possession, scaled to a per-100 rate because that's the convention for basketball rates. The predictors are indicators, one per player, coded +1 on offense, −1 on defense, and 0 when she isn't playing.

points = (sum of the 5 offensive players' ratings) − (sum of the 5 defensive players' ratings) + baseline

A least-squares fit on that gives every player a coefficient: her effect on scoring with the other nine on the floor already accounted for. This is Adjusted Plus-Minus. Structurally it's a multiple regression with about 540 predictors.

Why OLS (Ordinary Least Squares) blows up

Fit it with plain least squares and the output is garbage. Coefficients of +40 and −35 land on players who are obviously ordinary.

The cause is strong correlations between players: two players who are almost always on the floor together produce nearly identical columns, and the regression has no way to tell whose credit is whose. It resolves that by giving one a huge positive coefficient and the other a huge negative one, which fits the training data exactly as well as any other split. The WNBA has this worse than the NBA: rosters run 11 or 12 deep instead of 15 and a season is 44 games instead of 82, so the same five players share the floor constantly.

The response is also brutally noisy. One possession returns 0, 2, or 3 points. Predicting that from a lineup is close to estimating a coin's bias from a single flip.

Ridge Regression

Ridge fixes this by making large coefficients expensive. OLS minimizes the sum of squared residuals; ridge minimizes that plus a penalty term.

minimize: (sum of squared residuals) + λ × (sum of squared coefficients)

λ sets the trade-off. Large λ crushes everything toward zero, small λ gives back OLS and its garbage. It's the bias–variance trade-off: take on a little bias to kill a lot of variance, and land closer to the truth on average.

Shrink toward the box score, not toward zero

Plain ridge pulls every player toward zero, meaning league average. That wastes information, because I already know something about each player from her box score: points, rebounds, assists, steals, turnover rate, usage.

So I fit a box-score prior first, predicting each player's rating from counting stats alone, and point the ridge penalty at that instead of at zero.

minimize: (sum of squared residuals) + λ × (sum of squared distances from the box-score prediction)

Now, a player with few possessions falls back on her box score rather than on "average," and a player with thousands can pull away from it. That's the core of the method, and it's sort of like Bayesian inference: the box score is the prior, the possessions are the likelihood, and λ decides how much evidence it takes to move you.

Accounting for Defense. Box scores can't see defense. There's no counting stat for a shot that never got taken. Leonie Fiebich grades −1.30 from her box score and +3.41 once possessions are included, because teams simply score less when she's out there.

Rating to wins

The rating is in points per 100 possessions. Two constants turn it into wins, and I estimated both from WNBA data instead of borrowing the NBA's.

For points per win, regress each team's season win percentage on its average scoring margin. The slope works out to about 31.8 points of margin per win. I left the intercept free as a sanity check and it came back at 0.5000, which is what it has to be if the fit is sound: a team with zero point differential wins half its games.

Replacement level is the second constant. Value should be measured against a freely available player rather than against average, and that baseline lands at −2.98 points per 100.

WAR = (minutes / 1590) × (rating + 2.98)

The 1590 isn't a third constant I had to estimate. It falls out of the other two. A rating is points per 100 possessions, so multiply it by the possessions a player was on the floor for, divide by 100, and divide by points per win:

WAR = (rating + 2.98) / 100 × possessions played / 31.77

Possessions played is just minutes × pace, and WNBA pace is 79.92 possessions per 40 minutes, or 1.998 per minute. Substitute that in and rearrange, and every constant collapses into one number:

1590 = 100 × 31.77 / 1.998 = 100 × points per win / possessions per minute

1590 is larger than any WNBA player's season minutes; the busiest player in a recent full season was Kelsey Plum at 1,525 in 2025. That is fine, because it is a unit conversion rather than a share of a season, and a 44-game schedule just means nobody reaches a full unit of it (the WNBA does not have their equivalent of Wilt Chamberlain averaging more than 48 minutes per game yet!). Running the same identity on NBA numbers, about 30 points per win and 100 possessions per 48 minutes, gives 1440, within about 2.5% of the 1475 that Stephen Noh's NBA model uses. Neither number was fit to the other, so the agreement is a sign the derivation is right.

Two players from the 2026 table make that concrete. A'ja Wilson is the best player in the model. DeWanna Bonner is a useful veteran sitting almost exactly at the league median rating, and the two play similar minutes, so almost all of the gap between them comes from the rating rather than from playing time.

It also shows how much minutes matter. Cut Wilson to Bonner's rating and hold her minutes fixed and she would produce 1.87 wins, not 8.94. Nobody is valuable in the abstract; they are valuable for as long as they are on the court.

Wins to dollars

This is where the WNBA stops resembling the NBA, and where copying the NBA approach would give the wrong answer. Under the 2026 CBA a team has a $7.0M cap, has to carry 12 players, and can't pay anyone below $270K. That means $3.24M is committed before anyone makes a single decision. The money a team actually chooses how to spend is the remaining $3.76M.

Dividing the league-wide discretionary pool by total wins above replacement gives about $228K per win. Pricing off the full cap instead gives $424K, which makes nearly every rotation player look like a bargain and throws away the resolution you need in a league this compressed.

value = $270,000 + WAR × $227,879

The CBA sets the maximum as a share of the cap rather than a fixed number: 17% standard and 20% supermax, so $1.19M and $1.4M in 2026. Against a $270K minimum that's a band of four or five to one. The NBA's is roughly forty to one. 17 of 164 players here are worth more than anyone is allowed to pay them, and that gap is surplus the rules hand to their team.

Does it predict anything?

One of the first things you learn building models is not to mix training and testing data. A model is only predictive if it can predict data it has never seen; score it on the data it was fit to and you are measuring overfitting. So I fit on every season up to some year, then predicted the next season's games, which the model has never seen. There were nine test seasons, scored on average error in predicted game margin.

Information usedAvg error (points)
No player information at all13.57
Box scores only12.84
Full model12.37

The gain holds in all nine seasons, so it's real. It's also small. Basketball games are mostly noise, and no rating system is going to change that.

How this compares to DARKO

The best-known predictive rating in the NBA is DARKO, built by Kostya Medvedovsky. The WNBA doesn't have one, which is why I thought this would be interesting to build. DARKO is the more sophisticated system and this isn't a reimplementation of it, so what follows is what the two share and where they diverge.

Neither system treats a rating as a fresh measurement every season, and both size the update by how much you already knew, so a new game moves an unknown rookie a lot and a veteran with thousands of possessions barely at all. They are also both tuned to predict rather than to describe, which is what I actually wanted: the number that best summarizes what already happened is not the number that best forecasts what happens next.

The structural difference is what gets tracked over time. DARKO runs a Kalman filter, a running estimate that updates as evidence arrives, on each box-score statistic separately, each with its own decay rate and its own aging curve, then maps that projected stat line onto an impact number. I go the other direction: estimate impact straight from possessions and use the box score only as the thing ridge shrinks toward.

That has a real cost. Because DARKO models each statistic, it can tell you where a player's improvement came from: better shooting, fewer turnovers, more steals. Mine can't. It splits a rating into offense and defense and stops.

Rebuilding DARKO for the WNBA outright was never really on the table. The model is closed, so it would be a reconstruction from a description rather than a port, and more to the point the pieces that make it powerful answer problems I don't have in the same form. Daily updating matters for in-season NBA decisions, but contracts get signed in the offseason, so a rating that refreshes every morning buys nothing. Per-stat filtering needs a lot of games per player to estimate a decay rate for each statistic, and a WNBA season is 44 games.

What I took were the ideas: shrink toward a prior, weight recent evidence more heavily, tune for prediction instead of description. Recency-weighted pooling is roughly where a Kalman filter settles once it stops learning, and it beat every simpler alternative on the forward test above. A per-stat filter is the obvious next step if it ever turns out to be worth building.

Current limitations

These are the things most likely to make a number on this site wrong. Knowing about them is not the same as having fixed them, so they are listed rather than buried.

  • The cap past 2026 is a projection. The CBA doesn't schedule it; it's a share of league revenue, bounded to a 13% move in 2027 and 10% a year after that, so every projected season carries that uncertainty. The 2026 figures are contractual and exact, and available in a PDF from wnbpa.com.
  • Supermax eligibility here is an upper bound. Reaching 20% of the cap requires re-signing with your prior team, among other conditions, and nothing in my data records which team a free agent came from. I treat five or more years of service as eligible, which overstates it.
  • Replacement level rests on a convention: that a team of freely available players wins 25% of its games. Nobody fields that team, so the number can't be measured. Plausible alternatives from 20% to 30% move every dollar figure by roughly ±7%.
  • Defense is still only partly visible. Possession data recovers a lot of what box scores miss, but defensive estimates remain the noisiest part of the model.
  • The uncertainty bands are optimistic. The ± on each player's value assumes the box-score prior is correct, so it measures how well the possessions pin her down given that prior, not total uncertainty. It's also wide enough that neighboring players in the table can't be reliably ranked against each other.
  • Only 2026 salaries are loaded, so surplus is computed against this season's cap hit and players on multi-year deals aren't evaluated across the whole contract.
  • Past seasons are priced in today's money. The league table goes back to 2017, which is as far as possession data goes, and the ratings there fit with that season's data. The dollars are not. I have one CBA, the 2026 one, so a 2019 value answers "what would this be worth today", not what anyone was paid. Minutes are scaled to a 44-game season too, since the league played 34- and 22-game seasons in that stretch. Those two choices are why the contract columns disappear when you pick an earlier year.
  • 2026 is unfinished. The season is partly played, so minutes are scaled up to a full 44 games at each player's current rate and adjusted for games missed. A late-season change in role won't show up.
  • Small samples stay unreliable. A player with a few hundred possessions is mostly reporting her box-score prior back to you, and the rating admits this only through its error band, which is easy to miss.
