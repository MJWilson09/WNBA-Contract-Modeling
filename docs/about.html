How the model works

The method, the arithmetic, and what it can't do

The goal is a dollar figure: given what a player did on the court, what should she be paid? Getting there takes three steps — measure her impact, convert that impact into wins, and convert wins into money. Each step is simple on its own. The first one is where all the difficulty lives.

Step 1a: why you can't just count points

The obvious measure of impact is plus-minus: track the score while a player is on the floor. If her team outscores opponents by 8 points in her 30 minutes, she's +8.

This fails immediately, and for a reason statistics has a name for: confounding. A bench player who happens to share the floor with three All-Stars will post a glowing plus-minus. A good player on a bad team will look terrible. Plus-minus measures the lineup, not the player. What we want is the effect of one player holding her teammates and opponents fixed — and "holding other variables fixed" is exactly what regression does.

Step 1b: one variable per player

So we build a regression. Each row of data is a single possession — one team has the ball, tries to score, and the possession ends. There are about 143,000 of them in this model.

The outcome variable is points scored on that possession (scaled to a per-100-possessions rate, which is how basketball rates are conventionally expressed). The predictors are indicator variables — one for every player in the league. For each possession, a player gets +1 if she is on offense, −1 if she is on defense, and 0 if she isn't playing:

points = (sum of the 5 offensive players' ratings) − (sum of the 5 defensive players' ratings) + baseline

Fit that by least squares and each coefficient is that player's estimated effect on scoring, with all nine other players on the floor already accounted for. This is called Adjusted Plus-Minus. It is, structurally, a multiple regression with about 540 predictors.

Step 1c: the problem with that regression

Run it as plain least squares and the results are nonsense — coefficients of +40 and −35 for players who are obviously ordinary.

The culprit is multicollinearity: predictors that move together. Two players who are almost always on the floor at the same time produce nearly identical columns of data, and the regression cannot tell which one deserves the credit. It responds by assigning one a huge positive coefficient and the other a huge negative one — a combination that fits the data just as well as any other. This is worse in the WNBA than the NBA: rosters carry 11–12 players instead of 15, seasons are 44 games instead of 82, so the same five-woman groups play together constantly and there is less independent variation to learn from.

On top of that, the outcome is extraordinarily noisy. A single possession yields 0, 2, or 3 points. Predicting that from who is on the floor is like estimating a coin's bias from one flip — the signal is real but tiny relative to the randomness.

Step 1d: shrinkage, the actual fix

The solution is to tell the regression that extreme coefficients are implausible. Ordinary least squares picks coefficients to minimise the sum of squared residuals. Ridge regression minimises that plus a penalty on how large the coefficients are:

minimise: (sum of squared residuals) + λ × (sum of squared coefficients)

The tuning constant λ controls the trade-off. Large λ forces every coefficient toward zero; small λ recovers ordinary least squares and its nonsense. This is the bias–variance trade-off: we accept a little bias, pulling estimates toward a default, in exchange for a large reduction in variance. On net the estimates get closer to the truth.

Step 1e: shrink toward a better guess than zero

Standard ridge pulls unknown players toward zero, meaning "league average." But we can do better, because we already know something about every player from her box score — points, rebounds, assists, steals, turnover rate, usage.

So the model first builds a box-score prior: a prediction of each player's rating using only her counting stats. Then the ridge penalty pulls toward that instead of toward zero:

minimise: (sum of squared residuals) + λ × (sum of squared distances from the box-score prediction)

Now a player with few possessions defaults to a sensible box-score-based estimate rather than to "average," and players with lots of possessions can pull away from it where the evidence warrants. This is the core idea of the method, and it is Bayesian in spirit: the box score is a prior belief, the possessions are evidence, and λ sets how much evidence it takes to move you.

Where the model earns its keep. Box scores cannot see defense — there is no statistic for "the shot she prevented." Leonie Fiebich grades −1.30 from her box score alone and +3.41 once possessions are included, because teams simply score less when she is out there. That correction is invisible to counting stats.

Step 2: from rating to wins

A rating is in points per 100 possessions. To turn it into wins we need two numbers, both estimated from WNBA data rather than borrowed from the NBA.

Points per win. Regress each team's season win percentage on its average scoring margin. The slope says how many points of margin buy a win: about 31.8. As a check, that regression's intercept was left as a free parameter and came out at 0.5000 — a team that outscores opponents by exactly zero wins exactly half its games, which is what it must be if the model is sane.

Replacement level. Value should be measured against a freely available player, not against average. That baseline works out to −2.98 points per 100 possessions. Combining:

WAR = (minutes / 1590) × (rating + 2.98)

Step 3: from wins to dollars

Here the WNBA differs sharply from the NBA, and copying the NBA approach would be wrong. Under the 2026 CBA a team has a $7.0M cap, must roster at least 12 players, and cannot pay anyone below $270K. So $3.24M of that cap is already committed before any choices are made. The money a team actually allocates is the remainder — about $3.76M per team.

Dividing that league-wide discretionary pool by total wins above replacement gives roughly $228K per win. Using the full cap instead would give $424K and make nearly every rotation player look like a bargain, which destroys the resolution you need in a league this compressed.

value = $270,000 + WAR × $227,879

One consequence dominates the results: the maximum salary is $1.4M against a $270K minimum — a ratio of about 5, where the NBA's is about 43. 15 of 164 players are worth more than the maximum the CBA permits anyone to pay them. The gap between what they're worth and what they can be paid is surplus the rules hand to their team.

Does it actually predict anything?

A model that only describes the past is easy to fool yourself with, so the ratings are tested the hard way: fit on seasons up to some year, then predict the following season's game results, which the model has never seen. Nine test seasons, scored by average error in predicted game margin.

Information usedAvg error (points)
No player information at all13.57
Box scores only12.84
Full model12.37

The improvement is real — it holds in all nine seasons — but notice its size. Basketball games are mostly noise, and no rating system changes that. Be suspicious of anyone claiming otherwise.

How this compares to DARKO

The NBA's best-known predictive rating is DARKO, built by Kostya Medvedovsky. The WNBA has no equivalent, which is the reason this project exists. It is worth being precise about what the two share and where they genuinely differ — DARKO is the more sophisticated system, and this is not a reimplementation of it.

The same two instincts. Both refuse to treat a player's rating as a fresh measurement each season. Both lean on the idea that a new observation should move your estimate by an amount that depends on how much you already knew — heavily for an unknown rookie, barely for a veteran with thousands of possessions on record. And both are built to predict rather than to describe, which is a real design choice: the number that best summarises what already happened is not the number that best forecasts what happens next.

The central structural difference is what actually gets tracked over time. DARKO runs a Kalman filter — a running estimate that updates itself as evidence arrives — on each individual box-score statistic, separately, with its own decay rate and its own aging curve. It then maps that whole projected stat line onto an impact number. This model goes the other way: it estimates impact directly from possessions and uses the box score only as the target that ridge shrinks toward.

That difference has a real consequence. Because DARKO models each stat, it can say where a player's improvement is coming from — better shooting, fewer turnovers, more steals. This model cannot. It splits a rating into offense and defense and stops there.

Why not simply build DARKO for the WNBA? Partly because the model is closed, so it would be a reconstruction from a description rather than a port. But mostly because the pieces that make DARKO powerful are answers to problems the WNBA version does not have in the same form. Daily updating matters for in-season NBA decisions; contracts are signed in the offseason, so a rating that refreshes each morning buys nothing. Per-stat filtering needs a lot of games per player to estimate a decay rate for each statistic — and a WNBA season is 44 games, not 82.

The honest summary: this borrows DARKO's ideas — shrink toward a prior, weight recent evidence more, tune for prediction rather than description — without its machinery, because at WNBA sample sizes the machinery is not where the remaining error lives. Recency-weighted pooling is roughly what a Kalman filter settles into once it stops learning, and testing bears that out: it beat every simpler alternative on the forward test above. A per-stat filter would be the natural next step if it were ever worth building.

Current limitations

These are the things most likely to make a number on this site wrong. They are listed because they are known, not because they are resolved.

  • The CBA figures are press-reported, not read from the agreement. Every dollar output depends on the $7.0M cap, $1.4M max and $270K minimum. Reported caps often exclude benefits that count against the real one. Worse, the 2027–2031 schedule isn't public, so the projection years use a smooth interpolation between the 2026 and 2032 endpoints — an assumption, not a fact.
  • Replacement level rests on a convention. It assumes a team of freely available players would win 25% of its games. Nobody fields such a team, so this can't be measured. Plausible alternatives (20%–30%) move every dollar figure by roughly ±7%.
  • Defense is still only partly visible. Possession data recovers a lot of what box scores miss, but defensive estimates remain the noisiest part of the model. Treat a surprising defensive rating as a question rather than an answer.
  • The uncertainty bands are optimistic. The ± figure on each player's value assumes the box-score prior is correct, so it measures how well the possessions pin her down given that prior — not total uncertainty. It is also large: for a typical player the band is wide enough that neighbouring players cannot be reliably ranked.
  • Only 2026 salaries are loaded. Surplus is computed against this season's cap hit. Players with multi-year deals are not evaluated across the whole contract.
  • 2026 is incomplete. The season is partly played, so minutes are scaled up to a full 44 games at each player's current rate, adjusted for games missed. A late-season role change won't show up.
  • Small samples stay unreliable. A player with a few hundred possessions is mostly reporting her box-score prior back to you. The rating is honest about this only through its error band, which is easy to overlook.
